The hottest neighborhood is only part of the story.

THE QUESTION

A surface-temperature map tells us where a city is hot. It does not, by itself, tell us which neighborhoods are most vulnerable. My research brought environmental and demographic information together to examine that difference.

01

A different way to ask the question

I studied 369 Phoenix census tracts using Landsat imagery, land-cover indicators, and census demographics. Rather than treating temperature as the whole problem, I compared temperature-only priorities with a multidimensional view of neighborhood vulnerability.

02

When the rankings diverge

Only 17 of the 37 highest-priority tracts appeared in both rankings. That difference matters: a decision based only on surface heat can identify a substantially different set of neighborhoods from a decision that also considers their wider vulnerability.

03

Making the patterns interpretable

Clustering identified three vulnerability profiles, providing a way to discuss why neighborhoods differ and how cooling interventions might respond. The work connects remote sensing with the practical question of where—and on what basis—a city should focus its attention.

INSIDE THE ENGINEERING

One city. Three ways to read it.

  1. 01

    Environmental evidence

    Landsat surface-temperature information and land-cover indicators describe the physical environment. The census tract is the neighborhood-scale unit of comparison, allowing environmental patterns and demographic information to be considered across the same 369 areas.

  2. 02

    A change in the decision rule

    I compared a temperature-only ranking with a multidimensional vulnerability ranking. Examining which tracts enter the top 37 makes the practical consequence visible: the definition of priority changes the places a cooling program would consider first.

  3. 03

    Profiles that explain differences

    Clustering identified three vulnerability profiles. These groupings give the analysis another layer beyond a ranked list: they help explain how neighborhoods differ and why one uniform intervention may not address every pattern of vulnerability.

Surface temperature, demographic vulnerability, and intervention priority answer related but different questions. The study compares those perspectives; it does not measure the effects of a completed cooling program.

CONTEXT & FURTHER READING

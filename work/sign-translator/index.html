THE QUESTION

Making science education more accessible led me to a difficult research question: how can language connect to coordinated movement? Sign-Translator explores that question while keeping generated motion distinct from linguistically validated American Sign Language.

01

More than a sequence of hand poses

Signing coordinates the hands, body, face, gaze, and timing. I’m exploring how to represent those channels together, connect them to language, and carry the result into an articulated avatar. Each stage presents a different problem: recovering motion, learning its structure, and checking what a generated sequence actually communicates.

02

From a synthetic baseline to continuous motion

The initial runnable system connects a spatiotemporal graph network, a language–motion contrastive objective, and conditional diffusion on synthetic motion. The newer branch works with continuous video-derived motion and part-wise discrete codes. Keeping those experiments distinct makes it possible to compare methods without treating a system diagram as evidence that every component is complete.

03

When a better loss produced less movement

One continuous diffusion experiment reached a promising denoising loss while producing nearly static motion. Adding a velocity loss did not resolve the collapse. That result pushed the experiments toward discrete motion tokens and more direct checks of body, hand, and head dynamics—a reminder that the training objective and the behavior we care about can diverge.

INSIDE THE ENGINEERING

Language has to survive the whole pipeline.

  1. 01

    Put the motion on one clock

    The continuous-data branch aligns front and side How2Sign video using landmark motion, then calibrates the cameras, triangulates positions, and fits joint rotations. Reprojection and rigid-bone checks constrain the reconstruction. These are video-derived estimates, with provenance retained, rather than motion-capture ground truth.

  2. 02

    Learn a vocabulary of movement

    A part-wise residual vector quantizer represents body, left hand, right hand, and face motion with discrete codes. The selected continuous-generation experiment predicts these codes from text plus spatial-locus conditioning. Separate channels preserve distinctions that a single stream of hand coordinates would miss.

  3. 03

    Test what the score is measuring

    An earlier retrieval evaluator scored motion with zeroed rotations surprisingly well: clip duration was leaking the answer. Fixed two-second windows, randomized offsets, and a duration-only control made that shortcut testable. A useful metric must respond to motion content, not just clip length.

The continuous training experiments extend the original synthetic baseline. Generated motion still requires independent linguistic evaluation before it can support a claim of understandable ASL.

THE QUESTION

Making science education more accessible led me to a difficult research question: how can language connect to coordinated movement? Sign-Translator explores that question from studio video to a rendered avatar, while keeping generated motion distinct from linguistically validated American Sign Language. No Deaf signer has yet judged its output, and the project says so wherever a result appears.

01

More than a sequence of hand poses

Signing coordinates the hands, body, face, gaze, and timing. The project represents all of them on one clock: eleven channels at 24 frames per second, covering body and hand rotations, head motion, 52 face coefficients, gaze, blink and contact. Each value carries a mark for whether it was actually observed, so missing data stays visible instead of being silently filled in.

02

Two cameras, one skeleton

The motion comes from studio video filmed from the front and the side. A pose estimator finds keypoints in each view; the views are synchronised by the motion of the wrists and elbows, the cameras are calibrated from the footage itself, and matching points are triangulated into 3D. One finding from this stage: with both cameras fixed on the signer, focal length cannot be recovered from the geometry alone. Adding a rigid-bone-length term to the calibration brought the focal error from 40–47% to within 1%.

03

A vocabulary of movement

A residual vector quantiser turns each frame into 32 discrete codes: four body parts (body, left hand, right hand, face) refined through eight stages each. Splitting by part preserves distinctions that a single stream of coordinates would blur, and moving from one codebook to the residual design cut reconstruction error from about 19° and 11 cm to about 8° and 2 cm.

04

What did not work, and what replaced it

The first plan was to generate a whole sentence of motion from text in one pass. On the project’s instruments, no end-to-end generator separated from blank motion. The current path is composition: generate each word separately, sample it sixteen times, keep the sample a recogniser reads best, and join the words with predicted timing. The output follows English word order, which is not ASL grammar, and the project states this plainly.

05

Reading the gauge

Generated motion is scored by translating it back to English and comparing with the source sentence, always beside controls: real signing, blank motion, shuffled order and the wrong sentence. On a fresh set of sentences read exactly once, composition scored 2.40, real signing 4.03 and blank motion 2.05. The milestone was 2.55. It was missed, and the result was published without a retry.

INSIDE THE ENGINEERING

Language has to survive the whole pipeline.

  1. 01

    Put the motion on one clock

    Front and side How2Sign video are aligned using landmark motion, then the cameras are calibrated, positions triangulated and joint rotations fitted. Reprojection and rigid-bone checks constrain the reconstruction. These are video-derived estimates, with provenance retained, rather than motion-capture ground truth.

  2. 02

    Predict codes, not coordinates

    An autoregressive transformer predicts the residual codes from text: a causal temporal model over frames, a byte-level text encoder, and a smaller depth model over the eight stages. Early free-running samples were recognised far less often than one-step predictions suggested; trimming, history dropout and masked losses closed most of that gap for isolated words.

  3. 03

    Test what the score is measuring

    An earlier retrieval evaluator scored motion with zeroed rotations surprisingly well: clip duration was leaking the answer. Fixed two-second windows, randomized offsets, and a duration-only control made that shortcut testable. The same suspicion is applied to every later instrument before it is allowed to grade the generator.

The release bar was fixed before any human rating: Deaf raters must understand generated sentences at least 70% of the time and within ten points of real signing. That study has been designed but not run. Until it is, nothing here supports a claim of understandable ASL.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT SHOW

The honest edges.

  • No Deaf signer has evaluated the generated motion. Automatic scores are a proxy, not comprehension.
  • The output follows English word order and does not model ASL grammar.
  • Model weights are not released, and the system is not a deployed translator.
